# Audio engineering: transcription and tuning

Last reviewed: 2026-11-09.

## Record and transcribe
The studio records the voice through an AudioWorklet in an AudioContext of its own (REC.2, `take.js`), not MediaRecorder: every captured frame has a context time, so the take is exactly the frames from its zero to Stop, stored as a 16-bit WAV at the context's rate (usually 44,100 or 48,000 Hz), the live channels averaged to mono. With Count in one bar first (on by default) the click plays one 4/4 bar at the project tempo (accented first beat, 1,600 Hz; then 1,000 Hz, the EXP.4 click) and the take's zero is the end of that bar; Click while recording keeps the click going at the tempo. The click goes to the speakers only and is never mixed into the take; on speakers the microphone can still hear it, so headphones are suggested. Stopping during the count-in records nothing. REC.3 moves a take by the round trip of microphone and output when something is heard while recording (measured with Measure, else the browser's estimate; see ARCHITECTURE "Recording over the song"), and the capture page records through the same worklet. While recording, the studio and the capture page can show the live pitch (SING.1, `pitch.js`): YIN on the microphone in an AudioWorklet beside the recording, at about 16 kHz (the input averaged over 3 samples at 44,100 or 48,000 Hz), 64 ms windows, a reading every 16 ms; within 10 cents on steady tones from 82 to 880 Hz in the Node tests; it only reads the input, so the take is unchanged. Imported audio and the context may run at 44,100 or 48,000 Hz. Tandem decodes audio, uses Web Audio's offline resampler to meet Basic Pitch's **22,050 Hz** input requirement, then maps note detections to portable MIDI pitches and second-based timestamps. A user encountered the original 48,000 vs 22,050 sample-rate failure; a regression test covers it.

For beatboxing, server analysis uses approximate energy peaks and spectral centroid to classify kicks/snares/hats. This is **not** an accurate trained classifier yet. Note confidence and correction UI require improvement.

A recording is read as its live channels (REC.1, `recordings.js` `liveChannels`): a channel 60 dB or more below the loudest is left out before the take is played, transcribed, polished, tuned, separated or exported, and the rest are averaged where one channel is needed. The Mac app's WebKit recorder writes a one-sided microphone as 2-channel audio with the right side all zero; without this the take played in the left ear only and was analysed at half level. Real stereo and a take that is silent throughout keep every channel. While Basic Pitch transcribes, the page gets one task between the model's windows (a second or two of audio each), so it can draw the progress.

## Playback and export: sampled instruments
The melody, harmony and drums play recorded General MIDI samples (`samples.js`, SND.1) rendered from the FluidR3_GM SoundFont (MIT license) and self-hosted in `vendor/gm-samples-1/`: mono 22,050 Hz MP3s, one root note every 3 semitones per instrument, dry (no reverb or chorus), velocity 100. The programs are piano 0 (Acoustic Grand Piano), marimba 12, strings 48 (String Ensemble 1), bass 33 (Electric Bass, finger), chip 80 (Lead 1, square), organ 16 (Drawbar Organ), guitar 24 (Acoustic Guitar, nylon) and flute 73; kick, snare and hat come from the SoundFont's standard drum kit. Sustained instruments (strings, chip, organ, flute) carry crossfaded loop points in `manifest.json` so a held note can last any length.
- A note plays its nearest root sample, pitched by playback rate (at most 1 semitone from the root inside the range), at a per-instrument level (`LEVELS`), and fades over the voice's release time when the key is released. Decaying instruments (piano, marimba, bass, guitar) are rendered for 2-4 s, so a note held longer than its recording fades out over the release time and ends with it instead of stopping abruptly (a bass or low piano note can still be clearly audible at that point); sustained instruments loop instead. When a key is released close to the end of its recording, the release fade is shortened to finish by the recording's end, so the note is never cut mid-fade.
- The same `playSample` and `playDrumSample` functions schedule live playback and the offline WAV export, so both sound alike. Samples for the parts a project uses are preloaded when it changes; Play waits up to 3 s for them and the WAV export up to 15 s; a part not loaded by then uses its oscillator voice, and the status line says so.
- If a part's samples are missing or still loading, it plays its oscillator voice from `voices.js` and the status line says so, for example "Playing composition with synthesized Flute, drums (samples failed to load: HTTP 404 ...)".
- The loop points assume the browser's MP3 decoder strips the encoder delay and padding (gapless decoding), which is checked in Chromium only; in Safari or Firefox a held looped note may click if it does not (issue #44).
- Not yet judged by ear on real speakers (see TESTING.md).

## Vocal polish vs vocal tuning
Polish (`polish.js`, on device since PERF.1): band-pass EQ, soft gating, compression and makeup gain (Natural/Studio), run in the browser on the decoded recording, so audio is not uploaded and there is no length cap beyond the device's memory. It is a sample-exact port of the former `/api/polish` SciPy DSP (`tests/fixtures/make_polish_fixture.py` keeps that reference). It can affect tone/level but does not fix singing pitch.

Tune (on this device by default, VOX.4, or `/api/tune`): **WORLD/PyWorld** vocoder, the same code compiled to WebAssembly for the device. Separate F0 estimation, spectral envelope and aperiodicity enable resynthesis with changed F0 while approximately retaining vowel/formant character. Corrected audio is a new WAV; the original is unchanged. Analysis and synthesis share one 10 ms frame period (`FRAME_PERIOD_MS`); before November 2026 synthesis used pyworld's 5 ms default, so tuned clips played at double speed and the second half was silent.

The browser calls Basic Pitch to detect vocal note targets and shows them in the pitch-contour editor (VOX.3, below). Vocal target detection has no length or note-count cap (PERF.3; `VOCAL_TARGET_LIMITS` in `transcription.js`); melody transcribed into the project keeps the 60 s / 128-note cap that `/api/chat` expects (`PROJECT_MELODY_LIMITS`).

**Hummed melody (TRANS.2).** Transcribe with Basic Pitch builds the melody from the sung pitch line, not from the model's note events (`contour.js` `sungNotes`): a note lasts as long as its pitch is held, a new note starts where the pitch steps to another, and a slide joins the note it settles on (or, at the end of a phrase, the note it falls from). The line is the contour read with a lower voicing threshold than the editor's (`MELODY_SALIENCE` 0.15 instead of 0.3, which missed more than half of a soft low hum), ignoring the bins below MIDI 36 where room noise peaks when nothing is sung, and taking the bin an octave or an octave and a fifth below the peak when it holds 75% of the peak's salience (a soft low hum can read loudest at its 3rd harmonic). It is median-smoothed over about one vibrato cycle, joined across dropouts under 150 ms (`MELODY_GAP`), and cut where it stays 0.7 semitone from the current note for 50 ms. 150 ms or more with the note activation (the model's note output at that pitch) under 0.25 is a breath and ends the stretch even when its noise reads at the sung pitch, so two notes of one pitch stay apart; a piece whose activation averages under 0.25 is fan noise in a breath, not a note; a piece under 0.1 s, or one moving at least 0.35 semitone at 3 semitones per second or faster, is a slide. Measured on a real recorded hum from the Mac app (9.8 s, low notes around G#2 to F#3 with wide slides), scored against a pyworld harvest F0 track (583 voiced 10 ms frames): TRANS.1's notes covered 386 voiced frames with 252 within half a semitone of the sung pitch, in 14 notes; TRANS.2's cover 463 with 285 within half a semitone, in 12 notes (the remaining frames are slides and the singer's own pitch between two semitones). On synthetic hums through the real model, made separately from the e2e fixtures (six 1.4 s notes with 40-cent vibrato, drift and fan noise at two levels; six 0.45 s repeats of one pitch with 250 ms breaths; six legato notes joined by 120 ms slides; seven low notes joined by 250 ms slides) every note sequence came out exactly; TRANS.1 gave 9, 18, 6, 10 and 18 notes. Two hums of one pitch less than 150 ms apart still become one note. The settings were checked one by one around these values: the synthetic sequences break first with a step of 0.6, an activation limit of 0.2 or less smoothing.

**Hummed melody (TRANS.1, replaced by TRANS.2 for Transcribe).** Transcribe with Basic Pitch merged the pieces of each sung note the same way as the vocal targets (`mergeFragments`, before the 128-note cap) with a 150 ms limit (`MELODY_GAP`) instead of 80 ms, because room noise drops the sung line and splits the model's notes for up to about 140 ms inside one hummed note. Before, the melody was Basic Pitch's notes as they came. Measured with the real model in headless Chromium on six 1.4 s hummed notes (40-cent vibrato at 5.5 Hz, 25 cents of drift, brown noise; `e2e/test_studio.py::hum_wav`): no noise 78 notes before, 6 after; light noise 68 before, 7 after (an 81 ms stray at the end); heavier noise 65 before, 6 after; very loud noise (0.25) 17 after. Six 0.45 s repeats of the same pitch with breaths of 250 / 150 / 100 ms came out as 26 / 23 / 21 notes before and 6 / 8 / 7 after: repeats with a breath of about 250 ms stay apart, but two hums of one pitch less than about 150 ms apart can become one note (Basic Pitch itself already joins some of them). The vocal tuning targets keep the 80 ms limit. If one 16 s tuning chunk would carry more than 128 targets (`/api/tune`'s hosted per-request cap; the Mac app allows 4096 per request), tuning stops with a "Too many note targets" error instead of silently dropping targets (delete low-value targets in the editor to get under it). Basic Pitch runs on the whole recording in one pass: a 3-minute take takes about 2 minutes in headless Chromium on a laptop CPU. Without WebGL (no GPU, a blocked GPU, or a browser without software WebGL) TensorFlow.js runs it on its pure-JavaScript `cpu` backend, many times slower (a 3-minute take had not finished after 240 s on a CI runner, #18); the progress then says it is slow because this browser has no WebGL graphics acceleration, and for Transcribe that the server listener is quicker but simpler (FIX.1, `transcription.js` `slowBackendNote`, from TensorFlow.js's `getBackend`, which the vendored bundle exports for it). The WORLD endpoint supports strength 0..1, optional `notes_json`, `vibrato` 0..1 (default 0) and `scale` (comma-separated pitch classes, used only without targets). With targets, each note moves by the difference between its target and its sung centre (the mean of its voiced frames within a semitone of their median; at most an octave), and each frame keeps `vibrato` of its deviation from that centre (the residual is capped at 2.5 semitones so a stray octave-error frame cannot be dragged far). Without targets it snaps voiced frames toward the nearest semitone, or the nearest note of `scale`. Each request is limited to a 25-second clip (in the Mac app's own window, DESK.2: 600 s and 4096 targets; FIX.1, #59: the window still sends a long take in sections of at most 2 minutes, `tuning.js` `LOCAL_SECTION_SECONDS`, because WORLD's memory grows with the length it analyses: a synthetic 10-minute sung take at 48 kHz through the Mac app's own `/api/tune` (`guarded_app` with the launch secret) peaked at 4,335 MB resident in one request and 1,582 MB in six 2-minute sections, 40 s against 37 s; `world_tuning.world_tune` alone peaked at 3,668 MB in one call, 2,103 MB in 3-minute sections, 1,305 MB in 2-minute and 811 MB in 1-minute ones; measured on 2026-11-15 on an Apple M5 at strength 1 to a C major scale); the browser (`tuning.js`) tunes longer recordings in 16 s chunks that overlap by 1 s, offsets each chunk's note targets to its own start time (when targets are set, a chunk with none keeps its original audio rather than being snapped, matching how audio outside the targets is left alone within a request), and joins the results with a 40 ms linear crossfade at the quietest point of each overlap. WORLD restarts each chunk at its own phase, so a long crossfade partly cancels; the short seam keeps any dip brief. A recording tunes in about one request per 15 s, so long takes can wait on the per-IP rate limit, which the browser retries after `Retry-After`. It does not separate voice from background music or reliably edit polyphonic sources.

### Known artifacts
- Pitch-detection errors, wrong octaves and note boundaries.
- Consonant, breath, transient and unvoiced regions; vibrato flattening when Keep vibrato is 0.
- Audible timbre changes and mechanical artifacts even with formant-aware synthesis.
- Short notes and blended pitches may not produce reliable targets.
- The contour editor edits one target pitch per note plus its time span; it does not draw free-hand pitch curves, edit drift within a note, or split/join notes by hand. The corrected line is a preview computed from Basic Pitch's contour (its legend says "approximate preview"); the server measures pitch with WORLD, so the result can differ slightly, especially at note edges.
- If every detected target is deleted, Tune is disabled and the editor says so until Undo or Reset note edits brings a target back. Without that, tuning would get no targets and snap the whole take to the key or nearest semitone.

## Pitch-contour editor (VOX.3)
`contour.js` (pure, Node-tested) turns Basic Pitch's contour output into a continuous sung pitch line: for each model frame (about 11.6 ms), the strongest of 264 salience bins (3 per semitone) marks the frame voiced when it reaches 0.3, and the salience-weighted mean of the bins around it gives sub-semitone pitch. Bin 1 is MIDI 21: with bin 0 every steady test tone read exactly 33 cents sharp; with the offset they read within 1 cent. Frame times use the bundle's own frame-to-time mapping.

Detect sung notes then merges the pieces Basic Pitch splits a vibrato note into (`mergeFragments`): neighbours less than 80 ms apart whose sung centre is within 0.6 semitone of the sung centre of the run so far become one target at the rounded sung centre, unless the sung line has a gap of 80 ms or more across them (a breath always separates notes). Each piece is about half a vibrato cycle long, so its own centre swings with the vibrato; until TRANS.1 every piece had to be within 0.6 semitone of every other, and a 40-cent vibrato with 25 cents of drift (pieces centred 56.6..57.3 for one A3) broke one note into several. On a synthetic five-note phrase with ±25-cent vibrato this turned 37 detected pieces into 6 targets (the five notes, plus one piece the detector carried into a 100 ms silence). Each target keeps Basic Pitch's mean note activation as its confidence (0..1). Targets under 40% are drawn dashed in amber, and every target's opacity follows its confidence. Confidence never leaves the browser; `/api/tune` receives only `m`, `t` and `d`.

The editor draws the sung line (grey) and the predicted corrected line (mint) for the current Pitch correction strength and Keep vibrato amount. `previewCorrection` mirrors `world_tuning._target_notes` and is checked against the server on a shared fixture (`tests/fixtures/target-correction.json`). Targets are sliders: drag one up or down to change its pitch, or drag its edges (on targets at least 30 px wide) to change where it applies; resizing never grows into a neighbouring target or past the take. With a key and scale chosen (chromatic, major, natural minor, major or minor pentatonic), drags and the arrow keys move by scale degree, Snap notes to key moves every target to the nearest scale note (ties go to the side nearer the sung pitch), and correcting without detected targets snaps to that scale. Delete or Backspace removes the focused target. Every editor change — drag, resize, key, snap, step, delete — is one undoable edit in the EDIT.1 history.

Keep vibrato defaults to 100% in the editor, so by default a target moves the note's centre and leaves its vibrato and drift alone; 0% gives the flat VOX.1 behaviour. The API default stays 0 so existing callers and the VOX.1 benchmark are unchanged. Whether 100% or a lower default sounds better on real singing is for VOX.2's listening test.

## Pitch-correction benchmark (VOX.1)
`tests/test_vocal_benchmark.py` runs with `python -m pytest` (about 20 s). It renders a sung phrase (C4 E4 G4 F4, each 0.8 s, sung 30-45 cents off) as a ten-harmonic synthetic voice at 16, 22.05, 44.1 and 48 kHz in four variants: steady, ±25-cent 5.5 Hz vibrato, breath noise at 25 dB SNR, and 150 ms glides between notes. Each clip goes through the real `/api/tune` route in process. The output is measured with an independent YIN pitch tracker in the test, not WORLD's own F0 analysis. Pitch is scored on voiced frames more than 120 ms from a note change.

| Check | Threshold | Measured (Nov 2026) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pitch error with note targets, strength 1: median / 90th percentile | ≤ 3 / ≤ 6 cents | 0.2-0.4 / 0.4-0.8 cents (from ~37-40 / 45-61 before) |
| Pitch error, nearest-semitone snapping (steady, noise), strength 1: median | ≤ 3 cents | 0.2-0.4 cents |
| Error reduction (before ÷ after, median) | ≥ 10× | about 100× |
| Strength 0.5 leaves this share of the original median error | 35-65% | 49-50% |
| Length | identical sample count | identical |
| Note transitions (pitch crossing halfway between notes) | moved ≤ 20 ms | ≤ 10 ms (one analysis hop) |
| Loudness change per note / whole clip | ≤ 1 dB / ≤ 0.5 dB | < 0.05 dB |
| Note targets beat snapping when sung 55-70 cents off (steady): median / 90th percentile | ≤ 3 / ≤ 6 cents | 0.2-0.3 / 0.5-0.6 cents (snapping without targets lands about 100 cents off, on the neighbouring semitone) |
| Strength 0, with and without targets | output PCM identical to input | identical |
| Keep vibrato 1 (vibrato case): each note's mean pitch / vibrato spread (std) kept | ≤ 5 cents from target / 80-120% of the input's | 1.3-3.0 cents / 98-103% |
| On-device engine (VOX.4) against the server, every case and rate, with and without targets: SNR of the difference / largest difference | ≥ 70 dB / ≤ 32 16-bit steps | 84-117 dB / 1-12 steps |

Every check above runs twice: on the server (`/api/tune`) and on the on-device engine (VOX.4: `worldtune.js` with the WORLD WebAssembly build, run by Node from `tests/world_device.mjs` on the same 16-bit samples). Both pass every threshold. The device engine is the same WORLD source (the commit pyworld 0.3.6 is built from) with the same options and the same F0 correction, so the two differ only by floating-point rounding between the compilers and their maths libraries.

The measured metrics for every case are written to `.pytest_cache/d/vox1/metrics.json` (or `$TANDEM_VOX_REPORT`). The benchmark found that `encode_wav` truncated with a 32767 scale while `decode_wav` divides by 32768, so every pass through the server lowered most samples by one 16-bit step; it now rounds on the 32768 scale and round-trips exactly.

Vibrato: with `vibrato=0` (the API default) correction is applied frame by frame, so strength 1 removes vibrato entirely and strength *s* keeps about (1 − *s*) of its depth; the rows above score pitch against the flat note and so reward that. With `vibrato=1` (the contour editor's default) the note's centre moves onto the target and its vibrato survives, which the Keep vibrato row checks. Which sounds better on a real voice is a listening question for VOX.2. The note centre is a mean, not a median: the median of a vibrato over a non-whole number of cycles was up to 6 cents off.

What it does not show: the fixtures are clean synthetic harmonics with known pitch, so WORLD's analysis is near ideal. They have no consonants, breaths, formant movement, octave errors or real-room noise, and nothing here measures timbre or artifacts by ear. Real-voice quality is VOX.2's blinded listening test.

## Voice separation (SEP.1)
In the Mac app, Separate voice from music splits a recording into the vocal and the accompaniment with Demucs' htdemucs model (`tandem_desktop/separation.py`); the vocal is then what Transcribe, Polish and Tune work on, and the full recording stays the original. It runs on Metal (MPS) when the Mac has it, else on the CPU.

**Acceptance check** (`tests/test_separation.py`, run with the real model by the `separation` CI job on CPU, and on the Mac with `TANDEM_TEST_MODEL_HOME`): the fixture (`tests/separation_fixture.py`) is a 9 s, 16-note melody sung by a WORLD-synthesized voice (vowel formants, vibrato, glides, a "la" at each note) over plucked chords, a bass line and drums that are louder than the voice. The server's transcriber (`analyze_melody`) is scored by melody recall: a note of the melody counts as found when transcribed notes of the same MIDI pitch cover at least half of its duration.

| Transcribed from | Recall (Mac, Metal) | Recall (CPU) | Required |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The mixture | 0.00 | 0.00 | at most 0.2 |
| The separated vocal | 0.81 | 0.75 | at least 0.6, at most 0.1 below the voice alone, and at least 0.4 above the mixture |
| The voice alone (no music) | 0.62 | 0.62 | reference: the transcriber's own ceiling on this voice |

Through the studio window (`e2e/test_separation.py`) the browser hands the server its own 48 kHz decoding of the take; recall there was 0.69 on Metal (required: at least 0.5, and at most 0.2 before separating). The stems also sum back to the recording (residual under 10% RMS) and correlate with the voice (above 0.8) and the band (above 0.9) they were made from.

**Speed:** a 3-minute song (the fixture repeated) separated in 24 s on Metal on the operator's Mac (Apple M5, while the machine was otherwise busy) and in 263 s on its CPU; loading the model takes under half a second. The first Separate also downloads the model (84 MB). Still to check by ear on the operator's Mac: real songs (a phone recording of a song playing in the room, an existing track), and how much of the band leaks into the vocal. On the fixture the separated vocal still carries the band at about -24 dB against the voice, and RoFormer models leave 10-21 dB less but are 12-41 times slower (ROFORMER.0, below). The recall above is one draw: Separate shifts the input at random each time, and eight runs gave 0.62-0.75 on Metal.

## Backing band (GEN.2)
In the Mac app, Generate band (in the Band track under Harmony) plays up to 30 s of a full band that follows the person's own melody, with Meta's MusicGen-Melody (1.5B parameters, `tandem_desktop/band.py`). The window sends the melody as audio from time zero: the melody notes rendered with their instrument, or the recording (the tuned take when there is one, else the separated vocal or the original). The model is conditioned on the melody's chroma (the strongest of the 12 pitch classes every 128 ms, computed the way transformers' `MusicgenMelodyFeatureExtractor` does) and on a text prompt: the style in the person's words, then the project tempo and key (`lo-fi band with brushed drums, 100 bpm, in C major`). The band starts at the project's zero, so it lines up with the notes and the recording; it plays a little under the parts (gain 0.8), is a part stem in Export stems and is in Export WAV and `mix.wav` unless muted. It runs on Metal (MPS) when the Mac has it.

**Melody-following check** (`tests/test_band.py::test_the_band_follows_the_melody_it_is_given`, real model, `TANDEM_TEST_BAND=1`): an 8 s C-major arpeggio melody and the same melody a tritone up each get an 8 s band (`pop band with piano, bass and drums`, 120 bpm). Each band's pitch-class profile (the share of frames whose strongest pitch class is each of the 12) is compared with both melodies' profiles by cosine similarity. Result on the operator's Mac: 0.99 and 1.00 with its own melody, 0.004 and 0.00 with the other one (required: own above other for both).

**Speed:** 8 s of band took 43-56 s on Metal on the operator's Mac (Apple M5, while the machine was otherwise heavily loaded), so a 30 s band takes about 3 minutes there; loading the model takes about 15 s. The first Generate band downloads the model (6.23 GB). Still to check by ear on the operator's Mac *(HUMAN)*: a 30 s band from a real hum, whether it sounds like a band playing the song, and the time it took.

## Sung harmony (HARM.3)
Sing it in my voice (Harmony voice track; guided Song step) moves the take onto the harmony voice's notes with the same WORLD tuning as Tune, on this device by default (VOX.4) or on the server, at strength 1. Each harmony note is first split where the sung notes begin and end (`tuning.js` `harmonyTargets`), because WORLD moves each target note by one offset from the pitch sung under it; the result is kept only inside the harmony notes (`keepSpans`, 10 ms fades). Notes outside MIDI 36-96 are left out and the status line says how many.

**Acceptance check** (`tests/test_vocal_benchmark.py::test_sung_harmony_follows_the_harmony_voice`, required: each note's median and the 90th percentile of all frames within 20 cents): every benchmark case at 16 and 44.1 kHz, on both engines, tuned to a line of thirds above the sung phrase and to a counter-line 5 to 10 semitones below whose notes each span two sung notes, with targets built by `harmonyTargets` itself. Measured on 2026-11-14: per-case median 0.13-0.49 cents, 90th percentile 0.33-1.16 cents, the worst note median 0.82 cents, the same on the server and the device. Through the studio (`e2e/test_sung_harmony.py`, device engine in Chromium) the three harmony notes of a synthetic four-note take came out -0.5, +0.2 and -0.1 cents from their targets in the exported stem, and silent where the harmony voice has no note.

What it does not show: the takes are clean synthetic harmonics, so a real voice moved a third or more will also carry WORLD's artifacts and keep the singer's formants at the new pitch; how natural it sounds, and how far it can move before it sounds artificial, is the operator's listening check *(HUMAN, the HARM.3 acceptance; docs/TESTING.md)*.

## Voice as an instrument (TIMBRE.1)
In the Mac app, Play my voice on it (the Voice as instrument track under Band) plays the recorded voice as a soprano saxophone, an electric guitar or a church organ with RAVE timbre transfer (`tandem_desktop/timbre.py`, the Intelligent Instruments Lab's models, CC-BY-NC 4.0). It sends the tuned voice when there is one, else the take, and the result is a new recording of the same length that plays from zero with the parts; the original stays. RAVE keeps the timing, pitch and loudness of the voice and replaces its sound, so a clean, dry, in-tune take gives the best result: tune it first. Other models in the same collection (magnetic resonator piano, voices, birds, water) were left out: the piano model did not follow the sung pitch (196, 262 and 392 Hz came back at about 96 and 131 Hz), and the others are not instruments.

**Pitch and timing check** (`tests/test_timbre.py::test_the_instrument_plays_the_sung_line_in_time_and_in_tune`, real models, `TANDEM_TEST_TIMBRE=1`): a 5 s synthetic sung line (ten notes C4-C5 with 5.5 Hz vibrato and harmonics, after 0.5 s of silence) through each model. Result on the operator's Mac: every note within 0.2 semitones of the sung pitch for all three (required: 8 of 10 within a semitone); the instrument starts 7 ms (sax), 9 ms (guitar) and 45 ms (organ, a slower attack) after the voice (required: within 60 ms); 5.5 s took 0.7-1.2 s on the CPU, model load included.

Still to judge by ear on the operator's Mac *(HUMAN, the TIMBRE.1 acceptance)*: at least two of the instruments on real singing in the native window (whether it sounds like the instrument playing the person's line, and which takes work best).

## Magenta RealTime 2 (MRT.0 spike)
A spike outside the app (`tools/mrt-spike/spike.py`, commands in its README): Google's Magenta RealTime 2 (`magenta-rt` 2.0.3, Hugging Face revision `010aa0dc`), the exported MLX models, on the operator's Mac (Apple M5, 32 GB). The input is the melody and harmony of `tests/fixtures/notation-project.json` (F major, 96 bpm, 10 s, looped to 30 s) as the model's piano roll (128 pitches every 40 ms: 2 on a note's first frame, 1 while held), the style `pop band with piano, bass and drums`, drums on, the library's defaults (temperature 1.3, top-k 40, style guidance 3, notes guidance 1, seed 0). The model is stepped one 40 ms frame at a time with that frame's notes, the way live control would drive it. There are two controls: the same notes a tritone up (B major, which shares two of its seven pitch classes with F major), and the output's own notes two bars (5 s) later, which keeps the key and takes away the timing.

Measured on 2026-11-15, each 30 s of 48 kHz stereo, for the notes (n) and for the tritone (t):

| Model | Time for 30 s | Step, mean / p95 (real time: 40 ms) | Frame match with its own notes | ...with the other notes | Profile cosine, own / other |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `mrt2_small` (230M) | 17.4 s (n), 14.0 s (t) | 23 / 38 ms, 19 / 25 ms | 0.73, 0.75 | 0.14, 0.15 | 0.77 / 0.30, 0.90 / 0.25 |
| `mrt2_base` (2.4B) | 45.5 s (n), 37.0 s (t) | 61 / 80 ms, 49 / 55 ms | 0.76, 0.66 | 0.13, 0.22 | 0.85 / 0.29, 0.78 / 0.41 |

The frame match is the share of frames with notes whose strongest chroma bin (an 8192-point spectrum folded into 12 pitch classes from 55 Hz to 4.2 kHz, one frame per 40 ms, in `spike.py`) is one of the pitch classes sounding then, at the best lag up to 1 s: 80 ms (two frames) for the small model and 40 ms for the base model against their own notes (lag 0 is 0.01-0.02 lower). The measure itself finds lag 0 for tones that play the notes exactly and 200 ms for the same tones 200 ms late (`tests/test_mrt_spike.py`). The profile cosine is GEN.2's measure (the share of frames whose strongest pitch class is each of the 12, by cosine), here against the notes' own profile (each frame's sounding pitch classes, equally weighted) rather than against chroma of the melody as audio, so it is not directly comparable with GEN.2's 0.99.

**Following the notes in time, not only the key:** against its own notes two bars later, the frame match falls to 0.31 and 0.45 (small) and 0.37 and 0.35 (base), against 0.66-0.76 in time, while the profile cosine is unchanged (it ignores time). So the music follows each note and chord as it comes, not just the key; the profile cosine alone could not show that.

**Memory and start-up:** an 8 s run peaked at 1.3 GB (memory footprint; 1.7 GB resident) with `mrt2_small` and 3.6 GB (4.1 GB resident) with `mrt2_base`, one model at a time; loading and warm-up took 0.8-1.9 s. Nothing is played aloud: the spike only writes WAV files.

**Files and loading (MODEL.1):** every file is safetensors (weights and state), `.mlxfn` (an MLX graph exported with its weights, loaded with `mx.import_function`), TFLite (the MusicCoCa style encoders, run by `ai-edge-litert`) or a SentencePiece model; nothing is a pickle and nothing in `magenta_rt` loads one. The Hugging Face API gives each file's size and SHA-256 (LFS), so they can be pinned the way MODEL.1 pins its files. `mrt2_small` is 465 MB, `mrt2_base` 2.79 GB, and the shared resources 1.38 GB (MusicCoCa 958 MB, SpectroStream 418 MB); the full training checkpoints (1.1 and 9.8 GB) are not needed. The `.mlxfn` files at this revision need MLX 0.31.1 exactly: MLX 0.32.4, which pip picks, refuses them (`[import_function] Invalid string size`).

**Packages:** `magenta-rt[mlx]==2.0.3` with `mlx==0.31.1` resolves together with `requirements-models.txt` (pip dry run), but brings JAX, flax, orbax, recurrentgemma, google-cloud-storage, gcsfs, librosa and ai-edge-litert with it, even for MLX only. It needs Python 3.11 or later and Apple silicon for MLX.

**Live control:** reachable from the backend. The small model steps in 19-23 ms on this Mac, so a Python loop can change the notes every 40 ms frame and stay ahead of playback; the base model (49-61 ms a step) cannot here, as Google's table says (live 2.4B needs an M4 Pro, M2 Max or better). What the spike did not try is getting the frames to the page with low latency (a stream from the backend to an AudioWorklet), and the latency from a note change to its sound (the library states about 200 ms).

**Verdict:** add it beside the MusicGen band, don't replace it yet. Both sizes follow the project's own notes, chords included, frame by frame; the small model makes 30 s in 14-17 s where MusicGen takes about 3 minutes on the same Mac (measured while it was heavily loaded), and it is steered by the notes and chords themselves rather than by the melody's chroma; all under a license that allows distribution with attribution (LIC.1). Next: a band from the notes (MRT.1) as a second engine for the Band track, small model by default. Later, if the listening check holds up: styled parts (SND.1) and live accompaniment for a MIDI keyboard (EXP.4) or a take (REC.3), behind a streaming path to the page. Not for TIMBRE.1: it would play the notes, not the voice. Still to judge by ear *(HUMAN)*: the four WAVs beside a MusicGen band from the same melody (whether it sounds like a band playing the song, and whether the base model is worth its time). The stated limits stand untested here: mostly instrumental stock music in training, drums only on or off.

## Anticipatory Music Transformer (AMT.0 spike)
A spike outside the app (`tools/amt-spike/spike.py`, commands in its README): Stanford's Anticipatory Music Transformer (https://github.com/jthickstun/anticipation at `af373979`, Apache 2.0; weights `stanford-crfm/music-small-800k`, `music-medium-800k` and `music-large-800k`, Apache 2.0: GPT-2 models of 128M, 360M and 780M parameters) on the operator's Mac (Apple M5, 32 GB), PyTorch 2.15.0 on MPS and on the CPU. The input is the melody of `tests/fixtures/notation-project.json` (F major, 96 bpm, looped to 30 s) as *anticipated controls*: notes the model writes around and never writes itself, so the melody comes back unchanged by construction (controls never appear in what `generate` returns; the run keeps only the notes it wrote). The control input is the same melody a tritone up (B major). Each accompaniment is scored against its own melody and key ("own") and against the other run's ("other"): the share of its notes in the key, the share of the notes sounding over a melody note that are consonant with it (an interval of 0, 3, 4, 7, 8 or 9 semitones), and the share that, with the melody note, holds two notes of one of the key's seven triads (`tests/test_amt_spike.py` checks the three measures). `harmony.js`'s own chords and bass for the same melody are scored the same way (`spike.py baseline`).

**The rests:** with only the melody as controls, every model writes almost nothing but the rest token, about once a second (10 rests and 2 notes in 10 s for the small model): it learned that from the library's padding of silent stretches with one rest a second. Two remedies were measured: never sampling the rest token ("no rests"), and starting the model from `harmony.js`'s chords and bass for the first bar (2.5 s) and scoring only what it writes after them ("1-bar prompt"). "Band" allows only piano, fingered bass and drums (instruments 0, 33 and the drum kit); otherwise it may choose any of the 129.

**Findings:**
- **It follows the melody.** With the 1-bar prompt every size keeps to the key (0.99-1.00 against 0.10-0.17 for the other key) and sits on the melody's chords well above chance: 0.63-0.70 consonant and 0.69-0.90 sharing a triad, against 0.19-0.40 and 0.06-0.15 with the other melody. The medium model fits best (0.90 sharing a triad). None matches `harmony.js`'s 1.00, which places every chord on the melody by rule; what the model adds is variety (each seed writes a different part) and its own rhythm.
- **The prompt sets the band.** Started from `harmony.js`'s piano and bass, the model keeps to piano and bass: with any instrument allowed, it chose the same two in 15 of 18 runs and added a third (strings, nylon guitar, steel guitar, a little drums) in 3. The medium model's band and any-instrument runs wrote the same parts. So it is a continuation of `harmony.js`'s first bar, not an arrangement of its own.
- **No rests is not usable.** Without the rest token the parts lurch: one run is drums only (114 hits and no pitched notes), another is 424 drum hits, others are piano alone. Drums appear only by chance in the other variants, so the model is no drum part writer either.
- **Time.** `generate` in the library has no key-value cache (a full forward pass over up to 1024 tokens for every token), so the time grows with how dense the music is and varies up to fivefold between seeds: on MPS the small and medium models write 30 s in 11-42 s with the prompt (one dense small run took 80 s), and the large model in 41-175 s. The CPU is 3-13 times slower. A cache would cut this a lot, but it means rewriting the library's sampling loop.
- **Files and loading (MODEL.1).** The small and medium weights are only `pytorch_model.bin`, a pickle, at these revisions, and no safetensors version exists on Hugging Face. Each converts once (`spike.py convert`: `torch.load(weights_only=True)`, without the unused `token_out_embeddings` and the tied `lm_head`, to `model.safetensors`) and then loads with `use_safetensors=True` with no missing or unexpected keys and the same logits (largest difference 0.0). But MODEL.1 never unpickles, so the app could use them only if the converted files were hosted somewhere pinned by size and SHA-256 (publishing them is the operator's decision), or by converting on the Mac with the restricted unpickler at install, which breaks MODEL.1's rule. Only the large model (`model.safetensors`, 3.12 GB at `e206a88d`) is usable as published, and it is the slowest.
- **Packages.** The library is installed `--no-deps` (its requirements pin transformers 4.29.2 and matplotlib; generating needs neither) and runs with torch 2.15.0, transformers 5.22.2, safetensors 0.8.0, tqdm, numpy and mido, so it fits beside `requirements-models.txt` as it is. It would still have to be vendored or pinned by commit, because it is not on PyPI.

Nothing is played aloud: `render.py` writes each seed-0 MPS result as a Tandem project played through the studio's own Export WAV with the recorded General MIDI samples (18 WAVs, 32-34 s, 44.1 kHz mono, peaks 0.28-0.47).

**Verdict:** keep `harmony.js` only for now; don't adopt the model. It does write accompaniment that follows the melody and never touches it (ADR-004), but only when `harmony.js` starts it, it fits the melody's chords less often than the rules do, it writes no useful drums, its only weights MODEL.1 can load as published are the large model's (41-175 s for 30 s on this Mac), and making it fast means rewriting its sampling loop. If the listening check finds the variety worth that, the shape to try is a Vary accompaniment button in the Mac app: the medium model, converted files hosted pinned, `harmony.js`'s first bar as the prompt, the result as the harmony part in one edit. No ADR, since nothing is adopted. Still to judge by ear *(HUMAN)*: the WAVs (medium and large, 1-bar prompt) beside `harmony.js`'s chords and bass, and whether the model's part sounds like playing along or like noodling.

**Weights and MODEL.1.** `rmvpe.onnx` is an ONNX graph (opset 10, input `[1, 128, time]` log-mel), not a pickle: it fits MODEL.1's pinned size and SHA-256 as published, though running it would add ONNX Runtime to `requirements-models.txt` (the `rmvpe.pt` beside it is a pickle). PESTO's default `mir-1k_g7.ckpt` (534,664 bytes) is a Lightning pickle that `torch.load(weights_only=True)` refuses (it holds an `omegaconf` `ListConfig`), so MODEL.1 could not load it; the older `mir-1k.ckpt` loads with `weights_only=True`. - **Contour and Transcribe: keep Basic Pitch.** RMVPE is the best tracker here on hard audio: on the separated voice it found 99% of the sung frames with 3.1% gross (Basic Pitch's VOX.3 settings 84% and 4.0%), and in the mix it still follows the voice (11% gross against 90%), and on the CPU it took 24 ms per second of audio against Basic Pitch's 713 in the browser. But it gives a pitch line and no notes, where Basic Pitch gives both from one run (TRANS.2's melody and VOX.3's targets), it is no more precise on clean singing (1-2 cents against 1.5-3.5), and at 362 MB it could only be a Mac app model. If real takes (VOX.2) show Basic Pitch losing the line on separated or mixed singing, the shape to try is RMVPE as the Mac app's sung line after Separate, beside Basic Pitch's notes.
- **Benchmark scorer: keep YIN.** It is the most precise of the six on VOX.1's clean synthetic phrases (0.2-1.7 cents median, under 4 cents at the 90th percentile), which the benchmark's 3-cent thresholds need; RMVPE's 20-cent bins and PESTO's 1-11 cents are too coarse to score them.

What it does not show: these are synthetic voices with known pitch and one synthetic mix; no real singing, room, breath or consonant noise beyond what the fixtures make, and no listening. Nothing is played aloud.

## Vocal separation models (ROFORMER.0 spike)
Three RoFormer vocal models with a checked licence were run beside HTDemucs on SEP.1's fixture: Kim Vocal 2, a mel-band RoFormer (`KimberleyJSN/melbandroformer`, 228 M parameters, MIT since its author relicensed it from GPL-3.0 on 2026-04-22); a small mel-band RoFormer (`Aname-Tommy/Mel_Band_Roformer_small`, 51 M, MIT by its model card's tag); and anvuew's band-split RoFormer (`anvuew/BS-RoFormer`, 51 M, GPL-3.0). The BS-RoFormer most tools use (viperx's `ep_317`) states no licence anywhere upstream and was left out. Scripts, pins and commands are in `tools/roformer-spike/`; `tests/test_roformer_spike.py` checks the clips, the measures and the overlap-add. Measured on 2026-11-15 on the operator's Mac (Apple M5, 32 GB), torch 2.15.0, one model at a time.

**Weights.** All three are `.ckpt` pickles holding a plain float32 state dict. `torch.load(weights_only=True)` (the restricted unpickler) takes each, and the tensors write once to safetensors and read back equal, so a model could ship as safetensors that MODEL.1 loads without pickle; the converted files are the checkpoint's size (913, 202 and 204 MB). The rotary frequencies every layer shares have to be written as separate copies.

**Each model is run eight times.** HTDemucs as the app calls it (`shifts=1`) shifts its input at random on every Separate, so the same mix gives a different vocal each time (samples differ by up to 0.016) and the melody recall moved from 0.62 to 0.81 between two runs. Every model separated the mix eight times, run *k* behind *k* times 37 ms of silence; the table gives the mean and the range. The RoFormers are deterministic, but they moved as much with the offset.

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| HTDemucs | 0.71 (0.62-0.75) | 0.78 (0.75-0.81) | 6.2 (6.0-6.3) | -24.5 (-24.6 to -24.2) | -43 |

Recall is SEP.1's measure: the server's transcriber on the separated vocal (0.00 on the mixture, 0.62 on the voice alone). SDR compares the separated vocal with the voice the mix was made from. "Band in the vocal" is the band's share once the vocal is fitted as voice plus band by least squares. The last column separates the band with nobody singing, so everything that comes out as the vocal is band.

Each run separates 2 s first, untimed, because the first pass on Metal compiles its kernels. Each model loads in under 2 s. The RoFormers run at their own configurations' chunk and overlap through MSST's overlap-add, in float32.

**Verdict: keep HTDemucs.**
- **The vocal is cleaner, as their authors say.** The band-split RoFormer leaves about 21 dB less band in the vocal than HTDemucs and doubles its SDR in dB. Kim's mel-band RoFormer leaves about 10 dB less band, with about 1 dB more SDR. With nobody singing, both RoFormers give near silence where HTDemucs lets the band through at -43 dB. The small mel-band model loses half the voice (correlation 0.63) and is no use.
- **Transcription is not better.** The server's transcriber found fewer notes in the RoFormers' vocals than in HTDemucs' (0.53 and 0.65 against 0.71). A cleaner vocal does not mean more notes for this heuristic transcriber, which is the measure SEP.1 set.
- **It costs too much for the Mac app's Separate.** Kim takes 12 times as long as HTDemucs for 3 minutes (125 s against 10 s on Metal) with 8.6 GB on the GPU, and is an 11-times-larger download. The band-split model takes 41 times as long and is GPL-3.0, which needs LIC.1's answer before it could ship.
- **If real songs show the band leaking into the vocal** (SEP.1's open check by ear), the shape to try is Kim Vocal 2, converted to safetensors, as an optional "cleaner, slower" Separate beside HTDemucs. No ADR, since nothing is adopted.
- **Found on the way:** Separate is not repeatable, because of HTDemucs' random shift. Two Separates of one take can give the melody a note or two apart. This is left as it is: the shift is Demucs' own default, and SEP.1's recorded 0.81 was one draw.

## Models to watch
Checked on 2026-11-15 and not filed as tasks; each line says why. The spikes that are filed are AMT.0, PITCH.0, WORDS.0 and ROFORMER.0 in `docs/ROADMAP.md`.
- **ACE-Step** (https://github.com/ace-step/ACE-Step, code and weights Apache 2.0) and **YuE** (https://github.com/multimodal-art-projection/YuE, Apache 2.0): whole songs with vocals from words and lyrics. They write a new song rather than build on the person's idea (ADR-004).
- **Seed-VC** (https://github.com/Plachtaa/seed-vc, GPL-3.0, archived) and **RVC** (https://github.com/RVC-Project/Retrieval-based-Voice-Conversion-WebUI, MIT): voice conversion. They could make the sung harmony (HARM.3) sound more like the person, but converting into another real person's voice needs their consent, and Seed-VC's GPL would bind the app's code; not before LIC.1.
- **Beat This!** (https://github.com/CPJKU/beat_this, code and weights MIT) and audio chord recognition: beats and chords from a recording. Useful only once importing existing songs matters; today the tempo and chords come from the person's own notes (ARR.1a, ARR.1c).
- **Transformers.js** (https://github.com/huggingface/transformers.js, Apache 2.0) and **ONNX Runtime Web** (https://github.com/microsoft/onnxruntime, MIT): runtimes, not models. Worth it only when a model should run in the browser; PITCH.0 measured PESTO in ONNX Runtime Web and kept YIN.
- **Lyria RealTime** (Google's Gemini API, experimental): live music steered by text prompts. It is a paid hosted service with no published price for it, steered by words rather than the person's notes, and it would send the session to Google; Magenta RealTime 2 (MRT.0) runs on the Mac instead.

## Research/evaluation plan
Build a consented test set of dry solo voices across ranges, devices, vowels, consonants, vibrato, quiet notes, slides and sample rates (16/22.05/44.1/48 kHz). Include known target MIDI and timings, silence and noisy negatives. Measure note error in cents, preservation of length, spectral artifacts, clicks and loudness. Conduct blinded listening tests against original and reference correction. Never claim “professional quality” merely because a deployment succeeds or unit tests pass.

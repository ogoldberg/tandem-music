# Audio engineering: transcription and tuning

Last reviewed: 2026-11-09.

## Record and transcribe
Browser MediaRecorder may record at 44,100 or 48,000 Hz. Tandem decodes audio, uses Web Audio's offline resampler to meet Basic Pitch's **22,050 Hz** input requirement, then maps note detections to portable MIDI pitches and second-based timestamps. A user encountered the original 48,000 vs 22,050 sample-rate failure; a regression test covers it.

For beatboxing, server analysis uses approximate energy peaks and spectral centroid to classify kicks/snares/hats. This is **not** an accurate trained classifier yet. Note confidence and correction UI require improvement.

A recording is read as its live channels (REC.1, `recordings.js` `liveChannels`): a channel 60 dB or more below the loudest is left out before the take is played, transcribed, polished, tuned, separated or exported, and the rest are averaged where one channel is needed. The Mac app's WebKit recorder writes a one-sided microphone as 2-channel audio with the right side all zero; without this the take played in the left ear only and was analysed at half level. Real stereo and a take that is silent throughout keep every channel. While Basic Pitch transcribes, the page gets one task between the model's windows (a second or two of audio each), so it can draw the progress.

## Playback and export: sampled instruments
The melody, harmony and drums play recorded General MIDI samples (`samples.js`, SND.1) rendered from the FluidR3_GM SoundFont (MIT license) and self-hosted in `vendor/gm-samples-1/`: mono 22,050 Hz MP3s, one root note every 3 semitones per instrument, dry (no reverb or chorus), velocity 100. The programs are piano 0 (Acoustic Grand Piano), marimba 12, strings 48 (String Ensemble 1), bass 33 (Electric Bass, finger), chip 80 (Lead 1, square), organ 16 (Drawbar Organ), guitar 24 (Acoustic Guitar, nylon) and flute 73; kick, snare and hat come from the SoundFont's standard drum kit. Sustained instruments (strings, chip, organ, flute) carry crossfaded loop points in `manifest.json` so a held note can last any length.
- A note plays its nearest root sample, pitched by playback rate (at most 1 semitone from the root inside the range), at a per-instrument level (`LEVELS`), and fades over the voice's release time when the key is released. Decaying instruments (piano, marimba, bass, guitar) are rendered for 2-4 s, so a note held longer than its recording fades out over the release time and ends with it instead of stopping abruptly (a bass or low piano note can still be clearly audible at that point); sustained instruments loop instead.
- The same `playSample` and `playDrumSample` functions schedule live playback and the offline WAV export, so both sound alike. Samples for the parts a project uses are preloaded when it changes; Play waits up to 3 s for them and the WAV export up to 15 s; a part not loaded by then uses its oscillator voice, and the status line says so.
- If a part's samples are missing or still loading, it plays its oscillator voice from `voices.js` and the status line says so, for example "Playing composition with synthesized Flute, drums (samples failed to load: HTTP 404 ...)".
- The loop points assume the browser's MP3 decoder strips the encoder delay and padding (gapless decoding), which is checked in Chromium only; in Safari or Firefox a held looped note may click if it does not (issue #44).
- Not yet judged by ear on real speakers (see TESTING.md).

## Vocal polish vs vocal tuning
Polish (`polish.js`, on device since PERF.1): band-pass EQ, soft gating, compression and makeup gain (Natural/Studio), run in the browser on the decoded recording, so audio is not uploaded and there is no length cap beyond the device's memory. It is a sample-exact port of the former `/api/polish` SciPy DSP (`tests/fixtures/make_polish_fixture.py` keeps that reference). It can affect tone/level but does not fix singing pitch.

Tune (on this device by default, VOX.4, or `/api/tune`): **WORLD/PyWorld** vocoder, the same code compiled to WebAssembly for the device. Separate F0 estimation, spectral envelope and aperiodicity enable resynthesis with changed F0 while approximately retaining vowel/formant character. Corrected audio is a new WAV; the original is unchanged. Analysis and synthesis share one 10 ms frame period (`FRAME_PERIOD_MS`); before November 2026 synthesis used pyworld's 5 ms default, so tuned clips played at double speed and the second half was silent.

The browser calls Basic Pitch to detect vocal note targets and shows them in the pitch-contour editor (VOX.3, below). Vocal target detection has no length or note-count cap (PERF.3; `VOCAL_TARGET_LIMITS` in `transcription.js`); melody transcribed into the project keeps the 60 s / 128-note cap that `/api/chat` expects (`PROJECT_MELODY_LIMITS`).

**Hummed melody (TRANS.2).** Transcribe with Basic Pitch builds the melody from the sung pitch line, not from the model's note events (`contour.js` `sungNotes`): a note lasts as long as its pitch is held, a new note starts where the pitch steps to another, and a slide joins the note it settles on (or, at the end of a phrase, the note it falls from). The line is the contour read with a lower voicing threshold than the editor's (`MELODY_SALIENCE` 0.15 instead of 0.3, which missed more than half of a soft low hum), ignoring the bins below MIDI 36 where room noise peaks when nothing is sung, and taking the bin an octave or an octave and a fifth below the peak when it holds 75% of the peak's salience (a soft low hum can read loudest at its 3rd harmonic). It is median-smoothed over about one vibrato cycle, joined across dropouts under 150 ms (`MELODY_GAP`), and cut where it stays 0.7 semitone from the current note for 50 ms. 150 ms or more with the note activation (the model's note output at that pitch) under 0.25 is a breath and ends the stretch even when its noise reads at the sung pitch, so two notes of one pitch stay apart; a piece whose activation averages under 0.25 is fan noise in a breath, not a note; a piece under 0.1 s, or one moving at least 0.35 semitone at 3 semitones per second or faster, is a slide. Measured on a real recorded hum from the Mac app (9.8 s, low notes around G#2 to F#3 with wide slides), scored against a pyworld harvest F0 track (583 voiced 10 ms frames): TRANS.1's notes covered 386 voiced frames with 252 within half a semitone of the sung pitch, in 14 notes; TRANS.2's cover 463 with 285 within half a semitone, in 12 notes (the remaining frames are slides and the singer's own pitch between two semitones). On synthetic hums through the real model, made separately from the e2e fixtures (six 1.4 s notes with 40-cent vibrato, drift and fan noise at two levels; six 0.45 s repeats of one pitch with 250 ms breaths; six legato notes joined by 120 ms slides; seven low notes joined by 250 ms slides) every note sequence came out exactly; TRANS.1 gave 9, 18, 6, 10 and 18 notes. Two hums of one pitch less than 150 ms apart still become one note. The settings were checked one by one around these values: the synthetic sequences break first with a step of 0.6, an activation limit of 0.2 or less smoothing.

**Hummed melody (TRANS.1, replaced by TRANS.2 for Transcribe).** Transcribe with Basic Pitch merged the pieces of each sung note the same way as the vocal targets (`mergeFragments`, before the 128-note cap) with a 150 ms limit (`MELODY_GAP`) instead of 80 ms, because room noise drops the sung line and splits the model's notes for up to about 140 ms inside one hummed note. Before, the melody was Basic Pitch's notes as they came. Measured with the real model in headless Chromium on six 1.4 s hummed notes (40-cent vibrato at 5.5 Hz, 25 cents of drift, brown noise; `e2e/test_studio.py::hum_wav`): no noise 78 notes before, 6 after; light noise 68 before, 7 after (an 81 ms stray at the end); heavier noise 65 before, 6 after; very loud noise (0.25) 17 after. Six 0.45 s repeats of the same pitch with breaths of 250 / 150 / 100 ms came out as 26 / 23 / 21 notes before and 6 / 8 / 7 after: repeats with a breath of about 250 ms stay apart, but two hums of one pitch less than about 150 ms apart can become one note (Basic Pitch itself already joins some of them). The vocal tuning targets keep the 80 ms limit. If one 16 s tuning chunk would carry more than 128 targets (`/api/tune`'s hosted per-request cap; the Mac app allows 4096 per request), tuning stops with a "Too many note targets" error instead of silently dropping targets (delete low-value targets in the editor to get under it). Basic Pitch runs on the whole recording in one pass: a 3-minute take takes about 2 minutes in headless Chromium on a laptop CPU. The WORLD endpoint supports strength 0..1, optional `notes_json`, `vibrato` 0..1 (default 0) and `scale` (comma-separated pitch classes, used only without targets). With targets, each note moves by the difference between its target and its sung centre (the mean of its voiced frames within a semitone of their median; at most an octave), and each frame keeps `vibrato` of its deviation from that centre (the residual is capped at 2.5 semitones so a stray octave-error frame cannot be dragged far). Without targets it snaps voiced frames toward the nearest semitone, or the nearest note of `scale`. Each request is limited to a 25-second clip (in the Mac app's own window, DESK.2: 600 s and 4096 targets, so a take up to 10 minutes is tuned in one request); the browser (`tuning.js`) tunes longer recordings in 16 s chunks that overlap by 1 s, offsets each chunk's note targets to its own start time (when targets are set, a chunk with none keeps its original audio rather than being snapped, matching how audio outside the targets is left alone within a request), and joins the results with a 40 ms linear crossfade at the quietest point of each overlap. WORLD restarts each chunk at its own phase, so a long crossfade partly cancels; the short seam keeps any dip brief. A recording tunes in about one request per 15 s, so long takes can wait on the per-IP rate limit, which the browser retries after `Retry-After`. It does not separate voice from background music or reliably edit polyphonic sources.

### Known artifacts
- Pitch-detection errors, wrong octaves and note boundaries.
- Consonant, breath, transient and unvoiced regions; vibrato flattening when Keep vibrato is 0.
- Audible timbre changes and mechanical artifacts even with formant-aware synthesis.
- Short notes and blended pitches may not produce reliable targets.
- The contour editor edits one target pitch per note plus its time span; it does not draw free-hand pitch curves, edit drift within a note, or split/join notes by hand. The corrected line is a preview computed from Basic Pitch's contour (its legend says "approximate preview"); the server measures pitch with WORLD, so the result can differ slightly, especially at note edges.
- If every detected target is deleted, Tune is disabled and the editor says so until Undo or Reset note edits brings a target back. Without that, tuning would get no targets and snap the whole take to the key or nearest semitone.

## Pitch-contour editor (VOX.3)
`contour.js` (pure, Node-tested) turns Basic Pitch's contour output into a continuous sung pitch line: for each model frame (about 11.6 ms), the strongest of 264 salience bins (3 per semitone) marks the frame voiced when it reaches 0.3, and the salience-weighted mean of the bins around it gives sub-semitone pitch. Bin 1 is MIDI 21: with bin 0 every steady test tone read exactly 33 cents sharp; with the offset they read within 1 cent. Frame times use the bundle's own frame-to-time mapping.

Detect sung notes then merges the pieces Basic Pitch splits a vibrato note into (`mergeFragments`): neighbours less than 80 ms apart whose sung centre is within 0.6 semitone of the sung centre of the run so far become one target at the rounded sung centre, unless the sung line has a gap of 80 ms or more across them (a breath always separates notes). Each piece is about half a vibrato cycle long, so its own centre swings with the vibrato; until TRANS.1 every piece had to be within 0.6 semitone of every other, and a 40-cent vibrato with 25 cents of drift (pieces centred 56.6..57.3 for one A3) broke one note into several. On a synthetic five-note phrase with ±25-cent vibrato this turned 37 detected pieces into 6 targets (the five notes, plus one piece the detector carried into a 100 ms silence). Each target keeps Basic Pitch's mean note activation as its confidence (0..1). Targets under 40% are drawn dashed in amber, and every target's opacity follows its confidence. Confidence never leaves the browser; `/api/tune` receives only `m`, `t` and `d`.

The editor draws the sung line (grey) and the predicted corrected line (mint) for the current Pitch correction strength and Keep vibrato amount. `previewCorrection` mirrors `world_tuning._target_notes` and is checked against the server on a shared fixture (`tests/fixtures/target-correction.json`). Targets are sliders: drag one up or down to change its pitch, or drag its edges (on targets at least 30 px wide) to change where it applies; resizing never grows into a neighbouring target or past the take. With a key and scale chosen (chromatic, major, natural minor, major or minor pentatonic), drags and the arrow keys move by scale degree, Snap notes to key moves every target to the nearest scale note (ties go to the side nearer the sung pitch), and correcting without detected targets snaps to that scale. Delete or Backspace removes the focused target. Every editor change — drag, resize, key, snap, step, delete — is one undoable edit in the EDIT.1 history.

Keep vibrato defaults to 100% in the editor, so by default a target moves the note's centre and leaves its vibrato and drift alone; 0% gives the flat VOX.1 behaviour. The API default stays 0 so existing callers and the VOX.1 benchmark are unchanged. Whether 100% or a lower default sounds better on real singing is for VOX.2's listening test.

## Pitch-correction benchmark (VOX.1)
`tests/test_vocal_benchmark.py` runs with `python -m pytest` (about 20 s). It renders a sung phrase (C4 E4 G4 F4, each 0.8 s, sung 30-45 cents off) as a ten-harmonic synthetic voice at 16, 22.05, 44.1 and 48 kHz in four variants: steady, ±25-cent 5.5 Hz vibrato, breath noise at 25 dB SNR, and 150 ms glides between notes. Each clip goes through the real `/api/tune` route in process. The output is measured with an independent YIN pitch tracker in the test, not WORLD's own F0 analysis. Pitch is scored on voiced frames more than 120 ms from a note change.

| Check | Threshold | Measured (Nov 2026) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pitch error with note targets, strength 1: median / 90th percentile | ≤ 3 / ≤ 6 cents | 0.2-0.4 / 0.4-0.8 cents (from ~37-40 / 45-61 before) |
| Pitch error, nearest-semitone snapping (steady, noise), strength 1: median | ≤ 3 cents | 0.2-0.4 cents |
| Error reduction (before ÷ after, median) | ≥ 10× | about 100× |
| Strength 0.5 leaves this share of the original median error | 35-65% | 49-50% |
| Length | identical sample count | identical |
| Note transitions (pitch crossing halfway between notes) | moved ≤ 20 ms | ≤ 10 ms (one analysis hop) |
| Loudness change per note / whole clip | ≤ 1 dB / ≤ 0.5 dB | < 0.05 dB |
| Note targets beat snapping when sung 55-70 cents off (steady): median / 90th percentile | ≤ 3 / ≤ 6 cents | 0.2-0.3 / 0.5-0.6 cents (snapping without targets lands about 100 cents off, on the neighbouring semitone) |
| Strength 0, with and without targets | output PCM identical to input | identical |
| Keep vibrato 1 (vibrato case): each note's mean pitch / vibrato spread (std) kept | ≤ 5 cents from target / 80-120% of the input's | 1.3-3.0 cents / 98-103% |
| On-device engine (VOX.4) against the server, every case and rate, with and without targets: SNR of the difference / largest difference | ≥ 70 dB / ≤ 32 16-bit steps | 84-117 dB / 1-12 steps |

Every check above runs twice: on the server (`/api/tune`) and on the on-device engine (VOX.4: `worldtune.js` with the WORLD WebAssembly build, run by Node from `tests/world_device.mjs` on the same 16-bit samples). Both pass every threshold. The device engine is the same WORLD source (the commit pyworld 0.3.6 is built from) with the same options and the same F0 correction, so the two differ only by floating-point rounding between the compilers and their maths libraries.

The measured metrics for every case are written to `.pytest_cache/d/vox1/metrics.json` (or `$TANDEM_VOX_REPORT`). The benchmark found that `encode_wav` truncated with a 32767 scale while `decode_wav` divides by 32768, so every pass through the server lowered most samples by one 16-bit step; it now rounds on the 32768 scale and round-trips exactly.

Vibrato: with `vibrato=0` (the API default) correction is applied frame by frame, so strength 1 removes vibrato entirely and strength *s* keeps about (1 − *s*) of its depth; the rows above score pitch against the flat note and so reward that. With `vibrato=1` (the contour editor's default) the note's centre moves onto the target and its vibrato survives, which the Keep vibrato row checks. Which sounds better on a real voice is a listening question for VOX.2. The note centre is a mean, not a median: the median of a vibrato over a non-whole number of cycles was up to 6 cents off.

What it does not show: the fixtures are clean synthetic harmonics with known pitch, so WORLD's analysis is near ideal. They have no consonants, breaths, formant movement, octave errors or real-room noise, and nothing here measures timbre or artifacts by ear. Real-voice quality is VOX.2's blinded listening test.

## Voice separation (SEP.1)
In the Mac app, Separate voice from music splits a recording into the vocal and the accompaniment with Demucs' htdemucs model (`tandem_desktop/separation.py`); the vocal is then what Transcribe, Polish and Tune work on, and the full recording stays the original. It runs on Metal (MPS) when the Mac has it, else on the CPU.

**Acceptance check** (`tests/test_separation.py`, run with the real model by the `separation` CI job on CPU, and on the Mac with `TANDEM_TEST_MODEL_HOME`): the fixture (`tests/separation_fixture.py`) is a 9 s, 16-note melody sung by a WORLD-synthesized voice (vowel formants, vibrato, glides, a "la" at each note) over plucked chords, a bass line and drums that are louder than the voice. The server's transcriber (`analyze_melody`) is scored by melody recall: a note of the melody counts as found when transcribed notes of the same MIDI pitch cover at least half of its duration.

| Transcribed from | Recall (Mac, Metal) | Recall (CPU) | Required |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The mixture | 0.00 | 0.00 | at most 0.2 |
| The separated vocal | 0.81 | 0.75 | at least 0.6, at most 0.1 below the voice alone, and at least 0.4 above the mixture |
| The voice alone (no music) | 0.62 | 0.62 | reference: the transcriber's own ceiling on this voice |

Through the studio window (`e2e/test_separation.py`) the browser hands the server its own 48 kHz decoding of the take; recall there was 0.69 on Metal (required: at least 0.5, and at most 0.2 before separating). The stems also sum back to the recording (residual under 10% RMS) and correlate with the voice (above 0.8) and the band (above 0.9) they were made from.

**Speed:** a 3-minute song (the fixture repeated) separated in 24 s on Metal on the operator's Mac (Apple M5, while the machine was otherwise busy) and in 263 s on its CPU; loading the model takes under half a second. The first Separate also downloads the model (84 MB). Still to check by ear on the operator's Mac: real songs (a phone recording of a song playing in the room, an existing track), and how much of the band leaks into the vocal.

## Backing band (GEN.2)
In the Mac app, Generate band (in the Band track under Harmony) plays up to 30 s of a full band that follows the person's own melody, with Meta's MusicGen-Melody (1.5B parameters, `tandem_desktop/band.py`). The window sends the melody as audio from time zero: the melody notes rendered with their instrument, or the recording (the tuned take when there is one, else the separated vocal or the original). The model is conditioned on the melody's chroma (the strongest of the 12 pitch classes every 128 ms, computed the way transformers' `MusicgenMelodyFeatureExtractor` does) and on a text prompt: the style in the person's words, then the project tempo and key (`lo-fi band with brushed drums, 100 bpm, in C major`). The band starts at the project's zero, so it lines up with the notes and the recording; it plays a little under the parts (gain 0.8), is a part stem in Export stems and is in Export WAV and `mix.wav` unless muted. It runs on Metal (MPS) when the Mac has it.

**Melody-following check** (`tests/test_band.py::test_the_band_follows_the_melody_it_is_given`, real model, `TANDEM_TEST_BAND=1`): an 8 s C-major arpeggio melody and the same melody a tritone up each get an 8 s band (`pop band with piano, bass and drums`, 120 bpm). Each band's pitch-class profile (the share of frames whose strongest pitch class is each of the 12) is compared with both melodies' profiles by cosine similarity. Result on the operator's Mac: 0.99 and 1.00 with its own melody, 0.004 and 0.00 with the other one (required: own above other for both).

**Speed:** 8 s of band took 43-56 s on Metal on the operator's Mac (Apple M5, while the machine was otherwise heavily loaded), so a 30 s band takes about 3 minutes there; loading the model takes about 15 s. The first Generate band downloads the model (6.23 GB). Still to check by ear on the operator's Mac *(HUMAN)*: a 30 s band from a real hum, whether it sounds like a band playing the song, and the time it took.

## Voice as an instrument (TIMBRE.1)
In the Mac app, Play my voice on it (the Voice as instrument track under Band) plays the recorded voice as a soprano saxophone, an electric guitar or a church organ with RAVE timbre transfer (`tandem_desktop/timbre.py`, the Intelligent Instruments Lab's models, CC-BY-NC 4.0). It sends the tuned voice when there is one, else the take, and the result is a new recording of the same length that plays from zero with the parts; the original stays. RAVE keeps the timing, pitch and loudness of the voice and replaces its sound, so a clean, dry, in-tune take gives the best result: tune it first. Other models in the same collection (magnetic resonator piano, voices, birds, water) were left out: the piano model did not follow the sung pitch (196, 262 and 392 Hz came back at about 96 and 131 Hz), and the others are not instruments.

**Pitch and timing check** (`tests/test_timbre.py::test_the_instrument_plays_the_sung_line_in_time_and_in_tune`, real models, `TANDEM_TEST_TIMBRE=1`): a 5 s synthetic sung line (ten notes C4-C5 with 5.5 Hz vibrato and harmonics, after 0.5 s of silence) through each model. Result on the operator's Mac: every note within 0.2 semitones of the sung pitch for all three (required: 8 of 10 within a semitone); the instrument starts 7 ms (sax), 9 ms (guitar) and 45 ms (organ, a slower attack) after the voice (required: within 60 ms); 5.5 s took 0.7-1.2 s on the CPU, model load included.

Still to judge by ear on the operator's Mac *(HUMAN, the TIMBRE.1 acceptance)*: at least two of the instruments on real singing in the native window (whether it sounds like the instrument playing the person's line, and which takes work best).

## Research/evaluation plan
Build a consented test set of dry solo voices across ranges, devices, vowels, consonants, vibrato, quiet notes, slides and sample rates (16/22.05/44.1/48 kHz). Include known target MIDI and timings, silence and noisy negatives. Measure note error in cents, preservation of length, spectral artifacts, clicks and loudness. Conduct blinded listening tests against original and reference correction. Never claim “professional quality” merely because a deployment succeeds or unit tests pass.
